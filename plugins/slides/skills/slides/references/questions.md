# Asking before you build

Ask only with a question tool your environment offers (AskUserQuestion, request_user_input, or similar) and only when someone is there to answer. Otherwise (an automation, another agent calling you, "just make it") decide, build, and state your assumptions in one line.

**When:** read what they gave you first. A brief that still leaves two or more decisions open that would change the result earns ONE question call of at most four questions before you write anything. One open point you can default, a complete brief, or a small revision earns none.

**What to ask:** questions that come from their material, not a form. Name what you read ("your notes cover four topics"), the tension or gap in it, and the decision that most changes the deck, most decisive first. Never ask what the conversation already answers; default what the setting implies and say so. Generic intake questions (audience? length?) only for an empty brief.

For a deck, the material usually leaves open: which thread leads and what gets cut, what the room should do afterwards (decide, approve, just know), whose voice the slides speak in, and for a deck that will be read rather than presented, how dense it may be. The setting implies the rest: "Thursday's staff meeting" means their own team and about eight slides; ask about the look only when no brand was given, and then as two or three concrete directions. A missing number is a bracketed placeholder, not a question.

**Options are the real work:** two to four concrete directions for this deck ("lead with the reorg", not "narrative"), each differing on an axis you can name, never shades of one idea; labels of a few words that carry the choice; your recommendation first, marked "(Recommended)" (none on questions of fact). Allow several answers when the options can combine; one when they exclude each other (one length, one format). No "Other" or "you decide" options and no free-text-only questions.

**After:** treat the answers as decisions and restate them in your one-line assumptions; decide whatever they left open and say what you picked. A second round (at most four new questions) only if they ask for one or an answer opens a question you could not have asked before. Never re-ask.

## Example

They pasted Q3 planning notes (hiring paused through Q4, Platform and Growth merging under one lead, usage billing launching September 22, an SDK beta "sometime in October", three open questions at the end) and wrote "deck for Thursday's eng staff meeting".

1. "Your notes give the reorg, the hiring pause and both launches equal weight. What should Thursday lead with?" Options: The reorg (Recommended) · The two launches · The hiring pause.
2. "The notes end on three open questions. What is Thursday for?" Options: Decide them in the room (Recommended): one slide per question with your proposal · A heads-up: one closing slide with owners and dates · A pre-read: denser slides that stand alone.
3. "The notes are blunt ('we over-hired in Q2'). Keep that voice on the slides?" Options: Keep it (Recommended) · Soften it; the blunt lines move to speaker notes.
